01 / The crisis

Beijing, January 2013

The opening question is a concrete one: how high did the daily average rise at the U.S. Embassy monitor? Daily values reveal the episode without confusing an hourly peak with a 24-hour mean.

02 / A policy marker, not a causal boundary

The timeline matters

The national Action Plan was issued in September 2013. Its targets ran to 2017, beyond this dataset. Beijing has the longest record here; the other U.S. posts started later. A line beginning late is missing history, not zero pollution.

Every day on record

Beijing, one stripe per day

Each stripe is one day at the selected U.S. diplomatic-post monitor. Darker means a higher daily concentration. Look for recurring seasonal patterns and grey gaps; this chart alone cannot identify their causes.

Scorecard

Same years, every city: 2014 against 2015

2014 and 2015 are the first two calendar years in which all five U.S. posts have at least 325 valid days each. These are both post-plan years: the comparison describes early changes, not the policy effect. Missing days can still affect the means.

Three station means fell and two rose slightly. These are descriptive changes, without a significance test. Regional targets use a different spatial scope; a consulate reading cannot establish that a city or region met its target.

03 / Beyond the average

Did high-pollution days also become rarer?

A lower mean does not guarantee fewer extreme days. Compare the share of valid days above 150 µg/m³, and keep the missing-day counts in view.

The twist

Policy or weather? A limited wind check.

Wind composition can change a pollution average. Here we compare Beijing in two equal calendar windows: 2011–2012 and 2014–2015, leaving 2013 as a transition year. Grouping days by wind is a useful check, but it does not hold all weather conditions constant.

This sensitivity check uses identical pooled weights for matching month × wind categories with at least five days per period. It does not control temperature, humidity, rain, wind speed, economic activity or emissions. It is not the full weather-adjustment model in Liang et al. (2016), and cannot isolate a policy effect.

Can we trust the numbers?

Do nearby monitors tell a similar story?

We keep one U.S. diplomatic-post series per city as a consistent source. Nearby Chinese station columns provide a cross-check over overlapping valid days. Agreement in timing is useful evidence, but different locations need not record identical concentrations.

Correlation measures co-movement, not exact agreement. Signed bias can cancel positive and negative differences, so mean absolute error (MAE) is also reported.

Guangzhou's "City Station" column in the source file is an exact copy of the US series, so it is left out.

Verdict

Early improvement, an unfinished answer

The dataset stops in 2015, two years before the national plan's deadline. Five individual monitors cannot represent all of China, and a before/after pattern cannot establish causality. The defensible answer is early, uneven improvement at these monitors—not a final national victory.

What this means for Thailand

Measure first

Publish consistent station records and missing-data coverage so people can assess a trend. This is a practical lesson, not an estimated policy effect in Thailand.

Compare under the same weather

Separate seasonal and weather variation from policy claims. A wind-only grouping is a starting point, not complete adjustment.

Plan for still-air days

Track high-pollution days as well as annual averages. An improving mean can coexist with severe episodes.

Evidence boundary

What did each monitor actually cover?

The files contain hourly timestamps for 2010–2015, but many PM2.5 entries are NA. A timestamp is not a measurement. Only Beijing spans the entire six-year window.

Sources & method

References

Measurements: UCI dataset 394 (Chen, 2016; CC BY 4.0), also distributed on Kaggle. Calculations use the original UCI download.

Research context: Liang et al. (2016), doi:10.1002/2016JD024877. Their full meteorological adjustment is not reproduced here.

Policy: State Council Action Plan, September 2013. Regional 2017 PM2.5 reduction goals versus 2012: Beijing–Tianjin–Hebei ~25%, Yangtze River Delta ~20%, Pearl River Delta ~15%; Beijing annual concentration ~60 µg/m³. These are not five identical city targets.

Health references: WHO 2021 guidelines and WHO 2005/2021 comparison table. Annual PM2.5: 10/5; 24-hour: 25/15 µg/m³. The 24-hour recommendation is evaluated at the annual 99th percentile.

Map: Natural Earth via world-atlas.

How the data are read

Aggregation: daily means require ≥18 valid hours; monthly means require ≥15 valid days; annual means weight all valid days equally. These are project completeness rules, not a claim of regulatory certification. No missing values are filled. Values are rounded for display only.

Sensitivity checks: the matched-calendar comparison uses only month/day pairs with valid readings in both 2014 and 2015. Beijing's month × wind check keeps groups with at least five eligible days per period, then applies identical weights to both periods: the average of each group's share of retained days in each period. Neither check isolates a policy effect.

Station comparison: both monitors must independently pass the daily completeness rule. Correlation, signed bias and MAE use all paired valid days; the plot displays up to 400 sampled dates. The duplicated Guangzhou column is excluded. File hashes and calculation inputs are included in the downloaded data.
